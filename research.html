Research

My research so far has been on Text-to-SQL: translating questions asked in plain language into queries over a database. The thread running through it is what happens when the question does not have a single correct answer.

Problem

A Text-to-SQL system takes a question and a schema and returns a query. Evaluation almost always assumes there is one right answer. Ask a person for "the top customers last quarter" and you get a follow-up question instead — top by what, and which quarter?

Write this section yourself

Give one concrete ambiguous question against a small schema and show the two or three queries a reasonable person could write for it. A worked example does more than any description — a reader from another subfield will get the whole problem in ten seconds. Then say why ranking and picking the single most likely query is the wrong response.

Method

The method is training-free and treats the database itself as the evidence for disambiguation, narrowing a candidate set in four stages: enumerate the readings the question could plausibly have; prune the ones the database contents rule out; ground the spans that have only one surviving reading; then iterate only over the spans that remain genuinely underspecified. Grounding matters because every unresolved span multiplies against every other one, so fixing the certain ones early is what keeps the final set small.

Write this section yourself — the most valuable content on the site

Explain why the method is shaped this way. Why prune before grounding rather than after? What made you treat database contents as evidence instead of ranking with the model? What did you try first that did not work?

Design reasoning and dead ends are what a PI cannot get from your CV, and this is the closest thing to a writing sample you have. Two or three paragraphs.

Results

On AMBIQT, a benchmark built specifically for ambiguous questions, recall improved by 11.2% over training-free zero-shot baselines with an average of five candidate queries returned. The candidate count is the point: recall is trivially cheap if you return everything, so the size of the set matters as much as the number attached to it.

Add limitations — do not skip this

Where does the method underperform? What kinds of ambiguity does it miss entirely? What does the database scan cost on a large schema? Undergraduates almost never write a limitations section, and it reads as maturity rather than weakness. It is also the section most likely to make a PI want to talk to you.
