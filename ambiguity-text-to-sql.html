Ambiguity resolution for Text-to-SQL

DIAL Lab, SKKU4 months, 2026with a first-year Ph.D. student

The problem

A Text-to-SQL system takes a question in English and a database schema, and produces a query. The standard evaluation assumes there is one right answer. Often there isn't.

Write this section yourself

Give one concrete example of an ambiguous question against a small schema, and show the two or three queries a reasonable person could write for it. A worked example does more here than any amount of description — a reader from a different subfield will understand the whole problem from it in ten seconds.

Then say why the usual approach — pick the single most likely query — is the wrong response to this, in your own words.

The approach

The method is training-free: it runs on top of an existing model with no fine-tuning. Rather than resolving ambiguity by ranking, it treats the database itself as the disambiguating evidence, and narrows a candidate set in stages.

Write this section yourself — this is the most valuable content on the site

Explain why the method is shaped this way. Why prune before grounding rather than after? What made you treat database contents as evidence instead of ranking with the model? What did you try first that did not work?

Design reasoning and dead ends are what a PI cannot get from your CV, and are the closest thing to a writing sample you have. Two or three paragraphs is enough. Do not polish the failures out.

Results

On AMBIQT, a benchmark built specifically for ambiguous questions, the method improved recall by 11.2% over training-free zero-shot baselines while returning an average of five candidate queries — the point being that recall is cheap if you return everything, so the size of the candidate set matters as much as the recall number.

We evaluated across five Text-to-SQL benchmarks including BIRD, Spider, Ambrosia, and Kaggle DBQA, and reproduced eight baseline systems, four of which were positioned closely enough to compare against directly.

Add the limitations — do not skip this

Where does the method underperform? What kinds of ambiguity does it miss? What is the cost of the database scan on a large schema? What would you do differently with another two months?

Undergraduates almost never write this section, and it reads as maturity rather than weakness. It is also the section most likely to make a PI want to talk to you.
